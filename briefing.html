The Hormuz crisis, explained

Eight months after the war began, the strait is neither fully shut nor open. Here is what happened, what each side wants, and who is paying for it.

Where things stand

Ships are moving through Hormuz at roughly a fifth of their normal rate. Independent trackers counted about 12–17 crossings a day in late October, against 73–138 before the war depending on who is counting, and the UK’s maritime security agency put traffic about 70% below normal. Oil volumes have recovered more than ship numbers, because what does get through travels in large escorted tankers: the data firm Kpler estimated about 11.7 million barrels a day in October, against roughly 20 million before the war.

The two sides disagree about the order of things. According to Axios, the US wants nuclear concessions first; Iran wants the blockade lifted first. Talks mediated by Qatar continued in Doha into November.

“Open” and “closed” are contested words here. Iran has declared the strait open or shut several times, and US official figures on traffic are often higher than independent tanker counts. This page uses “restricted” where the picture is mixed and labels claims as claims.

      India is hit far harder than the US

      India buys most of its oil from abroad, and much of it came through Hormuz. The US is a net energy exporter and feels the crisis mainly at the pump.
